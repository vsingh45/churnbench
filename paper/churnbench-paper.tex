\begin{abstract}
Agentic systems in production answer questions over data that lives in several
places at once---a warehouse, an operational store, a rate-limited software-as-a-service
(SaaS) interface, a folder of contracts---and that data does not hold still. Licenses
get reassigned, users offboard, prices change, contracts renew. Most retrieval-augmented
generation (RAG) evaluations freeze a single corpus and ask whether the agent can find
things in it; they cannot ask the question a production operator actually cares about:
is the agent answering from the world as it is, or the world as it was when somebody
last built an index? We present a grounding architecture that places a dedicated data
layer between enterprise sources and the agent runtime---a semantic model across
sources, source-aware retrieval routing, and freshness-tiered caching---derived from
three design principles: computational efficiency, cost-aware architecture, and
purpose-built extract-transform-load (ETL) for agentic systems. To evaluate it we
built ChurnBench, an open-source benchmark that generates a simulated four-source
enterprise data fabric as a \emph{timeline} rather than a snapshot: every mutation is
written to a ground-truth ledger, so an agent's answer at time $T$ can be graded
against the true world state at $T$, making \emph{freshness error} a measured
quantity rather than an anecdote. Across

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\PARstart{A}{n} agent that answers enterprise questions has two jobs, and the
literature has concentrated on only one of them. The first job is reasoning: decompose
the question, call the right tools, compose an answer. The second job is quieter and,
in production, harder: make sure the data underneath that reasoning is the right data
--- drawn from the right source, joined correctly across systems that do not share
keys or vocabularies, and \emph{current}. Our experience building software asset
management (SAM) platforms over warehouse, NoSQL, SaaS, and contract-document sources
is that the second job dominates the failure modes. The agent is rarely wrong because
the model could not reason; it is wrong because a cache served last month's license
assignments, or a vector index built in January answered a question about March.

Prior work, including our own, has optimized the cost of the first job. Plan-time
tier routing assigns the right model to each subtask~\cite{par}; compile-time graph
transformation controls the cost of context carried between subtasks~\cite{tca}.
Both hold the data layer fixed. This paper completes the cost-and-correctness picture
from the other side: it holds routing fixed and varies \emph{where the data comes from
and how it is staged}. The question is whether the architecture of the data layer
beneath an agent moves accuracy, cost, and---the dimension no multi-source benchmark
currently measures---freshness.

The obstacle to studying this empirically is that freshness error is invisible in
existing evaluations. Benchmarks in the RAG and agent literature freeze a corpus,
generate questions against it, and grade retrieval or end-to-end answers~\cite{beir,
agentbench, bird}. A frozen corpus cannot produce a stale answer, by construction.
Recent work has begun to attack temporal drift for document corpora and agent
memory---Chronos evaluates model adaptation under continuous real-world knowledge
drift~\cite{chronos}, and MemStrata measures a stale-fact-error rate in agent memory
over evolving text facts~\cite{memstrata}---but the enterprise version of the problem
is architectural rather than textual: the staleness lives in caches, materialized
views, and vector indexes sitting over \emph{databases and APIs}, not in a single
corpus of prose. No existing benchmark injects controlled drift into a multi-source
structured-plus-unstructured fabric, and none measures what that drift costs an
agent in wrong answers or dollars.

We address both halves of the problem.

On the architecture side, we describe a grounding layer that sits between the
sources and the agent runtime and takes over the second job. It is built from three
design principles carried over from production practice: \emph{computational
efficiency} (lean agents that do not re-derive world state through tool-call loops),
\emph{cost-aware architecture} (a structured query is cheaper than a vector search
is cheaper than an LLM call, and the layer routes retrieval accordingly), and
\emph{ETL for agentic systems} (purpose-built pipelines that keep a unified semantic
model current, with cache tiers assigned by data volatility). Section~\ref{sec:framework}
states each principle as a decision procedure rather than a slogan, names the two
anti-patterns the principles exist to avoid---the \emph{tool-call storm} and
\emph{flatten-everything} RAG---and walks one task end to end through the layer.

On the measurement side, we built ChurnBench. Its central design choice is to
generate the world as a timeline, not a snapshot. A deterministic simulator plays
out $D$ days of enterprise life over a four-source fabric---a PostgreSQL warehouse,
a MongoDB operational store, a rate-limited mock SaaS interface, and a corpus of
synthetic contracts---and writes every mutation to an append-only ground-truth
ledger. The fabric at any timestamp $T$ is a fold over the ledger; evaluation runs
freeze the world at $T$ while caches and indexes may have been built at an earlier
$T'$. Because ground truth is derived from the ledger and never from the live
stores, an answer that was right at $T'$ and wrong at $T$ is detected and scored as
a \emph{freshness error}---distinct from a reasoning error, and attributable to the
grounding architecture rather than the model. Tasks are generated programmatically
from parameterized templates over four executive intents, so every question has a
provably correct answer at every timestamp, and no LLM-generated ground truth
contaminates grading. This continues the execution-grading methodology we have used
across four prior systems~\cite{par, tca, saasrat, gte}, for the same reason: a
grader model would fold its own mistakes into exactly the signal under study.

We evaluate four arms---naive per-query tool calling, classic single-index RAG, a
hierarchical schema-aware agent in the spirit of Protocol-H~\cite{protocolh} without
freshness machinery, and the grounding architecture---plus ablations that remove one
principle at a time. [DATA REQUIRED: one-sentence headline result.] We report a
drift-rate sweep locating the crossover points where each architecture stops being
rational, because an architecture paper that wins every row of every table has
usually chosen its tables carefully; we prefer to map the territory.

This paper makes three contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A grounding architecture for agentic systems over heterogeneous, drifting
enterprise data: a dedicated layer implementing semantic modeling, source-aware
retrieval routing, and freshness-tiered caching, stated as decision procedures with

\item An empirical evaluation on [DATA REQUIRED: N] execution-graded tasks showing

A note on lineage. Our earlier SaaS-rationalization work~\cite{saasrat} introduced
the contract-locked idle trap, a failure mode that is intrinsically temporal---a
cancellation window closing while a seat sits idle---but evaluated it on a frozen
synthetic estate. ChurnBench is the generalization that makes time itself the
experimental variable. Where that paper asked whether an agent reasons correctly
about temporal facts presented statically, this one asks whether the system serves
the agent facts that are still true.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{RAG Architectures and Their Evaluations}
Retrieval-augmented generation has moved from single-shot retrieve-then-generate
toward modular and agentic variants in which the system decomposes questions,
issues sub-queries, and iterates~\cite{ragsurvey}. The evaluation corpora
underneath this progress, however, remain overwhelmingly static and single-source:
BEIR~\cite{beir}, MS MARCO~\cite{msmarco}, and the multi-hop QA
sets~\cite{musique, twowiki} are web or Wikipedia snapshots. Practitioner reports
make the gap explicit: no public benchmark adequately represents enterprise
deployments, because most are built on web data alone~\cite{onyx}. WixQA moves to
an enterprise help-center corpus with a pinned knowledge-base snapshot~\cite{wixqa},
which sharpens grading but keeps the world frozen. Our benchmark differs on both
axes at once: multiple heterogeneous sources, and a world that moves.

\subsection{Agentic Frameworks and Enterprise Agent Benchmarks}
LangGraph~\cite{langgraph} and AutoGen~\cite{autogen} orchestrate multi-agent
workflows over explicit graphs and conversations respectively; both leave the data
layer to the application. Enterprise-flavored agent benchmarks have begun to
appear: AgentArch evaluates eighteen agentic configurations---orchestration,
prompting, memory, thinking tools---on enterprise tasks and finds even the best
models reach only 35.3\% on its complex tier~\cite{agentarch}; Protocol-H reports
that hierarchical supervisor--worker decomposition materially beats flat agents
when queries span SQL and documents~\cite{protocolh}; and DAG-orchestrated planners
target multi-modal hybrid data lakes with provenance tracking~\cite{daglakes}.
These works evaluate the architecture of the \emph{agent}; the fabric underneath
is a fixed snapshot in all of them, and none measures freshness or attributes cost
to the data-access strategy. We hold the agent architecture fixed and vary the
data layer---the complementary experiment.

\subsection{Temporal Drift, Staleness, and Freshness}
A recent line of work makes knowledge drift a first-class concern. Chronos
constructs a benchmark of real-world dynamic events from time-stamped evidence and
shows vanilla RAG and learning-based adaptation both struggle with temporal
consistency~\cite{chronos}. MemStrata defines a stale-fact-error rate---RAG serves
a superseded value 15--40\% of the time on evolving text facts---and eliminates it
with a deterministic supersession rule over a bi-temporal ledger~\cite{memstrata}.
Recency-prior scoring addresses freshness inside a single evolving
corpus~\cite{recency}. The FreshStack drift study asks the complementary
meta-question of whether static IR benchmarks stay valid as technical corpora
evolve~\cite{freshstack}. We share the bi-temporal-ledger instinct with
MemStrata but relocate the problem: their staleness lives in an agent's memory
over prose facts in one corpus; ours lives in the grounding architecture---caches,
materialized views, and vector indexes over databases, APIs, and documents. A
supersession rule over (subject, relation, object) triples has no purchase on a
stale materialized aggregate or an expired cache tier; the architectural version
of the problem needs an architectural treatment, and a benchmark on which it can
be measured. To our knowledge no prior benchmark occupies the intersection this
work targets: multi-source structured-plus-unstructured enterprise data,
controlled temporal drift, execution grading, and per-task cost.

\section{The Grounding Architecture}
\label{sec:framework}

Consider an agentic system answering questions over an enterprise fabric of $S$
sources spanning three modalities: relational stores holding history, document/NoSQL
stores holding current operational state, and external SaaS interfaces that are
authoritative but slow and rate-limited, plus an unstructured document corpus. The
system receives questions whose answers require one or more sources, and the fabric
mutates continuously underneath it. The grounding problem is to serve each retrieval
need from the right source, in the right modality, at acceptable cost, with data that
is still true.

The layer has four cooperating parts, each derived from one of three design
principles. We state each principle as the decision procedure an implementer would
follow, because a principle that cannot be operationalized is a slogan.

\subsection{Principle 1: Computational Efficiency}
\label{sec:p1}
\emph{An agent should never re-derive world state through iterated tool calls when
the grounding layer can stage it once.}

The failure mode this principle exists to prevent is the \textbf{tool-call storm}:
an agent that answers ``how many unused licenses in the Salesforce estate'' by
enumerating users through a rate-limited SaaS interface, one call per user. The
per-question latency and cost grow linearly with entity count, and the rate limiter
turns linear into catastrophic. The decision procedure: for each (source, access
pattern) pair the planner might need, classify it as \emph{stageable} (the answer
can be precomputed or bulk-extracted by a pipeline on a refresh schedule) or
\emph{inherently live} (the answer depends on this-second state or requires a
per-entity authoritative check). Stageable patterns are served from the layer;
only inherently live patterns reach the origin API at question time.

\subsection{Principle 2: Cost-Aware Architecture}
\label{sec:p2}
\emph{Retrieval operations have prices spanning orders of magnitude; route each
retrieval need to the cheapest operation that can answer it.}

A SQL aggregate over a warehouse costs effectively nothing per question. A vector
search costs an embedding call. Handing raw rows to an LLM to ``figure out'' costs
input tokens at the model's price, multiplied by every question. The anti-pattern
here is \textbf{flatten-everything} RAG: serializing every source---tables
included---into chunks in one vector index. It is cheap to build and blind twice
over: it discards the structure that makes aggregates exact (a chunked table cannot
be summed), and it freezes a copy of every source at index-build time, converting
all subsequent drift into silent wrongness. The decision procedure: structured
questions route to structured stores as generated queries; semantic-similarity
questions over prose route to the vector index; the LLM sees retrieved facts, not
raw dumps. The source-aware router implements this using the semantic model's
metadata about which entities and measures live where.

\subsection{Principle 3: ETL for Agentic Systems}
\label{sec:p3}
\emph{Agents need purpose-built pipelines that keep a unified, current semantic
model of the fabric---and the refresh schedule is a correctness parameter, not an
operations detail.}
\label{sec:freshness}

Classic warehouse ETL refreshes nightly because human analysts tolerate
day-old dashboards. An agent answering operational questions does not get that
tolerance, but refreshing everything continuously re-creates the tool-call storm
at the pipeline level. The resolution is \textbf{freshness-tiered caching}: each
entity class in the semantic model is assigned a tier by its volatility --- hot
entities (license assignments, user status in our SAM instantiation) get short
time-to-live (TTL) or change-data-capture treatment; warm entities (prices,
cost-center membership) get scheduled refresh; cold entities (contract terms,
vendor dimensions) refresh on event or on demand. The semantic model carries, per
entity class, its tier, its last-refresh timestamp, and its origin source; the
router consults these to decide whether a cached answer is servable or the
question must fall through to origin.

The decision procedure for tier assignment is empirical: measure (or estimate)
each entity class's mutation rate from the change stream, and set the TTL such
that the expected number of mutations within one TTL window is below a
correctness budget. Section~\ref{sec:results} [DATA REQUIRED] quantifies what
happens when this procedure is skipped---caching without freshness tiers converts
cost savings into wrong answers at a rate that scales with drift.

\subsection{What the Layer Explicitly Does Not Do}
The grounding layer does not plan, does not select model tiers, and does not
manage the agent's conversational memory. Those concerns are handled by, and
studied in, complementary work~\cite{par, tca, memstrata}; holding them fixed here
is what makes the data layer's contribution measurable in isolation.

\section{ChurnBench: A Drift-Aware Evaluation Instrument}
\label{sec:churnbench}

ChurnBench exists to make one quantity measurable that no current benchmark can
express: the rate at which an agentic system serves answers that \emph{were} true.
Its design follows from a single commitment: the world is generated as a timeline,
and ground truth is derived only from that timeline's ledger---never from the live
stores an arm might have corrupted, cached, or indexed.

\subsection{The Simulated Fabric}
The fabric has four sources chosen to reproduce the modality mix of a real
enterprise stack: (i) a PostgreSQL warehouse holding historical facts in a
star-adjacent schema (license purchases, consumption events, vendor and
cost-center dimensions); (ii) a MongoDB store holding current operational state
(users, active licenses, assignments, entitlements); (iii) a mock SaaS REST
interface that is deliberately slow (350\,ms injected latency) and rate-limited
(60 calls/min), so architectures that lean on live per-entity calls pay a
realistic price; and (iv) a corpus of [DATA REQUIRED: n] synthetic contract
documents rendered from the ledger's contract events, so renewal terms exist
only in prose. The domain is SAM---licenses, users, consumption, contracts---both
because drift is intrinsic to it (reassignment, offboarding, renewal, repricing
are the domain's daily physics) and because it extends an evaluation lineage we
can compare against~\cite{saasrat, tca}.

\subsection{Timeline Generation and the Ground-Truth Ledger}
A deterministic simulator (seeded NumPy generator; identical seeds produce
byte-identical worlds) plays out $D$ days. Each day it draws mutation counts
from Poisson streams---hires, offboardings, license reassignments, price
changes, contract renewals, cost-center moves---plus a dense append-only
consumption stream, and writes every event to the ledger with a monotonic
sequence number. Two parameters govern drift: the \emph{drift rate} (per-stream
Poisson means, swept in Section~\ref{sec:results}) and \emph{drift skew}, the
probability mass concentrated on the top decile of entities, reflecting the
empirical reality that a few products and users churn hardest---which is
precisely the regime where freshness-tiered caching should matter. The live
stores are projections: folding the ledger through timestamp $T$ materializes
Postgres, MongoDB, the SaaS store, and the contract corpus exactly as they
stood at $T$. Projection is idempotent and tested for it.

\subsection{Frozen-at-$T$ Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:frozen}
An evaluation run fixes two timestamps: $T'$, when an arm's caches and vector
indexes are built, and $T \ge T'$, when questions are asked and the live stores
stand. The interval $T - T'$ is the \emph{staleness window}, an independent
variable. Freezing the world during a run trades away one realism (drift
continuing mid-run) for exact reproducibility; we consider that the right trade
for a first instrument and return to live drift as future work.

\subsection{Task Generation}
Tasks are instantiated programmatically from parameterized templates over four
executive intents---spend visibility, savings opportunity, criticality, and
utilization---in three tiers: single-source, cross-source (requiring
SQL--MongoDB reconciliation), and cross-modality (requiring structured facts
joined with contract prose). Every template's gold answer is computed from the
ledger at $T$ by a non-LLM resolver, so grading is execution-based end to end;
no grader model's mistakes contaminate the freshness signal, for the same
reason we graded by execution in prior work~\cite{par, tca}.
[DATA REQUIRED: final task counts per tier/intent.]

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}
Four per-task measures: \emph{accuracy} against the ledger-derived gold answer;
\emph{cost} in USD (all model and embedding calls, exact token attribution per
our two-pass method~\cite{tca}); \emph{latency} end to end including injected
SaaS delays; and \emph{freshness error}. We define a freshness error as a task
outcome that is (a) wrong against the ledger at $T$ and (b) right against the
ledger at the arm's effective retrieval time for the facts it used---i.e., the
answer the arm gave was true of an earlier world. This definition separates
\emph{architectural staleness} from reasoning error: a wrong answer that was
never true of any world is a reasoning failure and scores against accuracy
only. We further attribute each freshness error to the stale artifact that
caused it (cache tier, vector index, or materialized view) where the trace
permits. [DATA REQUIRED: attribution coverage achieved in practice.]

\section{Discussion and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\textbf{Construct validity.} A skeptic might argue freshness error is just
accuracy error with a timestamp. The reply mirrors our position on memory
injection~\cite{tca}: the contribution is the attribution. A wrong answer that
was true of an earlier world implicates the grounding architecture and is fixed
by a refresh policy; a wrong answer that was never true implicates the model
and is not. Conflating them is how the problem stayed invisible.

\textbf{Internal validity.} The benchmark author also designed the winning
arm. Three mitigations: the drift-rate sweep is designed to locate where the
architecture \emph{loses}; the strong hierarchical baseline removes the
weak-baseline objection; and drift parameters are calibrated against published
churn statistics [CITATION NEEDED: employee-turnover and SaaS
license-reharvesting rates to anchor Poisson means] rather than chosen freely.

\textbf{External validity.} The estate is synthetic and the domain is SAM.
Synthetic data is what makes controlled drift and exact ground truth possible
at all, but prevalence claims do not transfer to real estates; the sweep
partially compensates by reporting behavior across regimes rather than one
configuration. Cross-provider validation is included in this study
[DATA REQUIRED: second-provider results] rather than deferred, breaking a
pattern our own prior work shares. Single agent framework (LangGraph) remains
a limitation.

\textbf{Relation to MemStrata.} The nearest prior work eliminates stale text
facts in agent memory via deterministic supersession~\cite{memstrata}. Our
staleness is architectural---a supersession rule over triples cannot refresh
a materialized aggregate or expire a cache tier---so the mechanisms are
complementary, not competing: their layer keeps the agent's remembered facts
current; ours keeps the facts the agent retrieves current.

\section{Conclusion}
Agentic systems fail in production less because models cannot reason than
because the data underneath the reasoning has moved. We presented a grounding
architecture that treats the data layer as a first-class component---semantic
modeling, source-aware routing, freshness-tiered caching---and ChurnBench, a
benchmark that generates enterprise data as a timeline with a ground-truth
